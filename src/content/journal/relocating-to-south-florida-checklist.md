Most relocation advice stops at "find a neighborhood you love." In South Florida, the decisions that shape your budget happen earlier: the flood zone, the insurance quote, the building's paperwork, and a few state deadlines that reward people who plan ahead. Here's the order I'd line things up in, with the official source for each step.

## 1. Check the flood zone before you fall for the house

Start with FEMA's Flood Map Service Center, the official public source for flood hazard maps[^1]. Type in the exact address, not just the neighborhood. Flood zones can change from one side of a street to the other.

Why it matters: if a property is in a Special Flood Hazard Area, that's where the mandatory purchase of flood insurance applies[^2]. In practice, that requirement usually surfaces when you finance the purchase, so expect your lender to ask about it. Even outside those zones, I ask clients to price flood insurance anyway. Water doesn't read maps.

**Timing tip.** A new flood policy through the National Flood Insurance Program typically has a 30-day waiting period before it takes effect. One exception: coverage bought in connection with a mortgage closing can take effect at closing[^3]. Don't leave the flood quote for the last week.

Also look up the address on the state's Know Your Zone map[^4]. Hurricane evacuation zones are separate from FEMA flood zones, and knowing yours is part of living here.

## 2. Get insurance quotes during your inspection period

Homeowners insurance in Florida is priced address by address, and the age and construction of the roof, windows and openings all matter. Get real quotes during your inspection period, not after it closes.

**Ask about wind mitigation.** Florida uses a Uniform Mitigation Verification Inspection Form to document wind-resistant features such as roof attachment and opening protection. Admitted insurers are required to accept a valid, properly completed form, and a completed form is valid for up to five years as long as the structure hasn't materially changed. A revised version of the form has been required for inspections since April 1, 2026[^5]. If the seller has a recent one, ask for it. If not, ordering one is a small cost that can change your premium.

**For condos,** your own policy covers the interior. The association's master policy covers the building. Ask for the master policy, its deductibles and any recent premium changes, because those flow straight into your dues.

## 3. Read the building, not just the unit

If you're buying a condo, Florida's post-2022 rules apply. Condo and co-op buildings of three or more habitable stories need a milestone inspection at 30 years of age and every 10 years after, and a local building department can require the first one at 25 years. Condo associations with buildings of three or more habitable stories must also complete a structural integrity reserve study (SIRS) at least every 10 years[^10].

Before you offer, ask for the milestone summary, the latest SIRS and its funding plan, the budget and reserve balances, any special assessments or loans, twelve months of board minutes, and the lease, rental and pet rules. For HOAs, read the rules and fees, and check whether there's a master association on top.

## 4. Plan your homestead filing around January 1

Florida's homestead exemption can reduce a primary residence's taxable value by up to $50,000. The first $25,000 applies to all property taxes, including school taxes. The additional exemption applies to assessed value over $50,000 and only to non-school taxes[^6][^7].

The key dates:

- **January 1.** To qualify for a given tax year, you need to own the home and make it your permanent residence by January 1[^7].
- **March 1.** File the application (Form DR-501) with your county property appraiser by March 1 of that tax year. Many property appraisers accept applications online[^7].

Homestead also unlocks the **Save Our Homes** assessment limit. After the first year with the exemption, the home's assessed value can't rise more than 3% or the change in the Consumer Price Index, whichever is less[^8]. If you're moving from one Florida homestead to another, you may be able to "port" part of that benefit to the new home, but you must establish the new homestead within three years of January 1 of the year you left the old one[^8].

**What that means for timing.** Closing in December versus January can shift when your homestead and Save Our Homes protection begin. Ask your county property appraiser how it applies to your situation, and loop in a tax professional for anything beyond the basics.

## 5. Make it official: license, title and registration

Florida gives new residents short windows. You must get a Florida driver license within 30 days of establishing residency. To title and register your vehicles, you need insurance from a Florida-licensed agent, and that has to happen within 10 days of establishing residency[^9]. Book the appointment before the moving truck arrives.

## 6. Test the commute before you sign

South Florida is long and narrow, and traffic shapes daily life. Some off-peak, free-flow drive estimates from the data behind my neighborhood guide[^11]:

- Brickell to Miami International Airport: about 25 minutes
- Las Olas to Fort Lauderdale-Hollywood International: about 15 minutes
- Brickell to downtown Fort Lauderdale: about 40 minutes

Rush hour runs longer, often much longer. Drive the route at the hour you'll actually travel it. If a Brightline station is close to where you'd live and where you'd work, the train can change the math entirely.

## Your pre-move checklist

1. Flood zone for the exact address, plus an evacuation zone check
2. Flood insurance quote, started early because of the 30-day wait
3. Homeowners or HO-6 quote, with a wind mitigation report if one exists
4. Condo or HOA documents read before the offer
5. A homestead plan built around January 1 and March 1
6. A Florida driver license within 30 days and vehicle title and registration within 10
7. The commute, driven at the hour you'll drive it

None of this is meant to scare anyone off. South Florida rewards people who do their homework, and every item above is easier before the contract than after it. This is general information, not legal, tax or insurance advice, so confirm the specifics with the agency or a licensed professional.
